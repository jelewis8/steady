# Focal Epilepsy: Clinical Knowledge Base

> **Scope**: This document is an educational reference for building a patient-facing epilepsy management application. It is not medical advice. Every treatment decision belongs to the patient's neurologist.

---

## 1.1 Scope and Definitions

### Focal (Partial) Seizures

Focal seizures originate in networks limited to one hemisphere of the brain. The ILAE 2017 classification replaced the older "simple partial / complex partial" terminology:

| Current Term | Former Term | Consciousness | Description |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Focal aware** | Simple partial | Preserved | Patient remains conscious throughout. May experience motor, sensory, autonomic, or psychic symptoms depending on the cortical region involved. |
| **Focal impaired awareness** | Complex partial | Impaired | Consciousness is altered or lost. Often accompanied by automatisms (lip smacking, hand fumbling, wandering). Most commonly originates in the temporal lobe. |
| **Focal to bilateral tonic-clonic** | Secondarily generalized | Lost | Begins focally, then electrical activity spreads to both hemispheres, producing a full tonic-clonic (convulsive) seizure. |

**Key distinction**: the term "focal" describes the *onset*, not the extent. A focal seizure that generalizes is still classified as focal-onset.

### Generalized Seizures (Contrast Only)

Generalized seizures involve both hemispheres from the start. Subtypes include absence, myoclonic, atonic, tonic, and tonic-clonic. They arise from different network mechanisms than focal seizures and often respond to different medications. This document does not cover generalized epilepsy in depth.

### Unknown-Onset Seizures

When there is insufficient information to classify the onset as focal or generalized, seizures are labeled unknown-onset. This is a provisional classification pending further diagnostic workup (ILAE, 2017).

### Seizure vs. Non-Epileptic Event

Not every paroxysmal neurological event is a seizure. Key differentials include:

- **Psychogenic non-epileptic seizures (PNES)**: Episodes resembling seizures but driven by psychological mechanisms, not abnormal electrical activity. Diagnosed via video-EEG monitoring. PNES and epilepsy can coexist in the same patient.
- **Syncope**: Loss of consciousness from cardiovascular causes (vasovagal, cardiac arrhythmia). May include brief myoclonic jerks that mimic seizure activity.
- **Transient ischemic attacks (TIA)**: Focal neurological deficits from transient vascular occlusion. Usually negative symptoms (weakness, numbness) rather than the positive symptoms typical of seizures.
- **Migraine with aura**: Visual, sensory, or language disturbances that spread gradually over minutes (vs. seizure auras lasting seconds).
- **Parasomnias**: Nocturnal events (sleepwalking, REM behavior disorder) that can mimic frontal lobe seizures.

Distinguishing these from true seizures requires clinical history, eyewitness accounts, EEG, and sometimes video-EEG monitoring.

## 1.2 Causes and Etiology

The ILAE 2017 classification defines six etiologic categories. These are not mutually exclusive — a patient may have both a structural lesion and a genetic predisposition.

| Category | Description | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Structural** | Identifiable brain abnormality on imaging | Hippocampal sclerosis (mesial temporal sclerosis), focal cortical dysplasia, brain tumor (low-grade glioma, DNET, ganglioglioma), stroke/infarction, traumatic brain injury, arteriovenous malformation, cavernous malformation, encephalomalacia |
| **Genetic** | Known or presumed genetic defect as direct cause | SCN1A mutations, DEPDC5-related focal epilepsy, tuberous sclerosis complex (TSC), familial focal epilepsy with variable foci. Note: "genetic" does not mean "inherited" — *de novo* mutations are common. |
| **Infectious** | Seizures resulting from CNS infection | Neurocysticercosis (the most common cause of acquired epilepsy worldwide), viral encephalitis (HSV, HHV-6), cerebral abscess, HIV, congenital infections (CMV, toxoplasmosis), tuberculoma |
| **Metabolic** | Metabolic disorder as primary seizure driver | Pyridoxine-dependent epilepsy, GLUT1 deficiency, mitochondrial disorders, porphyria, uremia, hepatic encephalopathy |
| **Immune** | Autoimmune-mediated encephalitis | Anti-NMDA receptor encephalitis, anti-LGI1 encephalitis, anti-CASPR2 encephalitis, anti-GAD65 antibody–associated epilepsy |
| **Unknown** | No identifiable cause after appropriate workup | A substantial proportion of focal epilepsies (~30–40%) remain cryptogenic |

### Common Structural Causes in Adults

- **Hippocampal sclerosis**: The single most common pathology in drug-resistant temporal lobe epilepsy. Involves neuronal loss and gliosis in the hippocampus. Often associated with a history of febrile seizures in childhood.
- **Focal cortical dysplasia (FCD)**: Localized malformation of cortical development. The most common cause of drug-resistant epilepsy requiring surgery in younger adults. MRI may be normal in FCD type I (detected only by histopathology).
- **Low-grade tumors**: Gangliogliomas, DNETs, and low-grade astrocytomas are slow-growing tumors that often present with drug-resistant focal seizures as the sole symptom.
- **Post-stroke epilepsy**: Cortical strokes carry approximately a 5–10% risk of developing epilepsy, typically within the first 1–2 years (ILAE, Epilepsia 2010).
- **Traumatic brain injury**: Risk depends on severity — penetrating injuries carry a 50%+ lifetime epilepsy risk; mild closed head injury confers little to no increased risk (AAN practice parameter).
- **Vascular malformations**: Arteriovenous malformations (AVMs) and cavernous malformations (cavernomas) are both epileptogenic. Cavernomas with hemosiderin rings are particularly so.

### Modifiable vs. Non-Modifiable Risk Factors

| Modifiable | Non-Modifiable |
|---|---|
| Alcohol use, sleep deprivation, medication non-adherence, head injury prevention (helmets, seatbelts), infection prevention, metabolic control | Age, sex, family history, prior febrile seizures, existing structural lesion, genetic predisposition |

## 1.3 Pathophysiology and Mechanisms

### Neuronal Hyperexcitability

Seizures arise from an imbalance between excitatory and inhibitory neural activity. At the cellular level:

- **Excitatory drive**: Glutamate acting on AMPA and NMDA receptors depolarizes postsynaptic neurons. Excessive glutamate release, impaired glutamate reuptake, or overexpression of excitatory receptors increases seizure susceptibility.
- **Inhibitory drive**: GABA (gamma-aminobutyric acid) acting on GABA-A and GABA-B receptors hyperpolarizes neurons. Loss of GABAergic interneurons (as occurs in hippocampal sclerosis) or dysfunction of GABA receptors reduces the inhibitory brake.
- **Ion channel dysfunction**: Mutations or acquired changes in voltage-gated sodium, potassium, and calcium channels alter neuronal firing thresholds. Sodium channel gain-of-function mutations cause neurons to fire repetitively; potassium channel loss-of-function reduces repolarization.

### Network Synchronization

A seizure is not a single neuron misfiring — it is pathological synchronization of a neuronal network. In focal epilepsy:

1. **Seizure focus**: A population of neurons in the epileptogenic zone fires abnormally and synchronously, generating interictal epileptiform discharges (IEDs) on EEG.
2. **Recruitment**: Surrounding neurons are recruited through synaptic and non-synaptic mechanisms (ephaptic interactions, gap junctions, extracellular potassium accumulation).
3. **Propagation**: Activity spreads along anatomical pathways. Temporal lobe seizures commonly propagate to the contralateral temporal lobe, frontal lobe, or insula.
4. **Generalization**: If inhibitory surround mechanisms fail, the seizure spreads bilaterally, producing a focal-to-bilateral tonic-clonic seizure.

### Semiology Maps to Anatomy

Because the seizure begins in a localized cortical region, the initial symptoms (the aura) reflect the function of that region. This is the basis of semiological localization:

- Motor cortex → contralateral clonic jerking
- Somatosensory cortex → contralateral tingling or numbness
- Visual cortex → visual hallucinations (simple: flashes/colors; complex: formed images)
- Temporal lobe → déjà vu, fear, autonomic symptoms, olfactory hallucinations
- Insula → visceral sensations, throat tightening, altered taste
- Supplementary motor area → bilateral asymmetric tonic posturing (fencing posture)

### Why Seizures Stop

Most seizures self-terminate within 1–3 minutes through:
- Activation of inhibitory circuits (depolarization block, GABA release)
- Adenosine release (an endogenous anticonvulsant)
- Extracellular pH changes (acidosis inhibits NMDA receptors)
- Neurotransmitter depletion

When these mechanisms fail, status epilepticus results — a medical emergency defined as a seizure lasting >5 minutes or recurrent seizures without recovery between episodes.

## 1.4 Symptoms, Precursors, and Semiology

### Prodrome (Hours to Days Before)

Some patients experience a prodromal period preceding a seizure by hours or even 1–2 days. This is not the aura (which is the seizure itself) but rather a pre-seizure state. Reported prodromal symptoms include:

- Mood changes: irritability, depression, euphoria
- Cognitive changes: difficulty concentrating, word-finding trouble
- Physical symptoms: headache, fatigue, insomnia or hypersomnia
- Autonomic symptoms: nausea, lightheadedness

Prodromes are reported by 6–47% of patients in published series. They are subjective and inconsistent, but when a patient recognizes a reliable prodrome, it can prompt preventive action (rest, medication timing, avoiding triggers).

### Aura (Seconds to Minutes — The Focal Aware Seizure Itself)

An aura is the conscious experience of a focal seizure. If the seizure progresses to impaired awareness, the patient may only recall the aura portion.

| Lobe | Aura Symptoms |
|---|---|
| **Temporal** | Déjà vu or jamais vu, epigastric rising sensation, fear or anxiety, olfactory hallucinations (often unpleasant), gustatory hallucinations, emotional experiences (intense familiarity, detachment, ecstasy in rare cases), auditory illusions |
| **Frontal** | Forced thinking, speech arrest, contralateral head/eye version, urge to move, brief bizarre behavior (hyperkinetic seizures — thrashing, pedaling, pelvic thrusting in sleep), vocalization |
| **Parietal** | Contralateral tingling, numbness, electrical sensation, feeling of a limb being distorted in size or position (body schema disturbance), vertigo |
| **Occipital** | Elementary visual hallucinations (flashing lights, colored spots, patterns), visual field deficits (hemianopia), eye deviation, eyelid fluttering |
| **Insular** | Throat or chest tightness, laryngeal constriction, visceral pain, altered taste, warmth or tingling spreading across the body |

### Motor Signs (Visible to Observers)

- **Clonic activity**: Rhythmic jerking of a limb or face, contralateral to seizure focus.
- **Tonic posturing**: Sustained contraction. Asymmetric tonic posturing (fencing) suggests supplementary motor area origin.
- **Automatisms**: Semi-purposeful repetitive movements — oroalimentary (lip smacking, chewing, swallowing), manual (fumbling, picking, fidgeting), ambulatory (walking, running). Most common in temporal lobe seizures with impaired awareness.
- **Versive movements**: Forced head and eye turning, usually contralateral to the focus. Late forced version (before secondary generalization) is a lateralizing sign.
- **Dystonic posturing**: Contralateral arm dystonia during temporal lobe seizures is a highly reliable lateralizing sign.

### Post-Ictal State

After a seizure ends, the brain enters a recovery period. Post-ictal symptoms are often more disabling than the seizure itself:

| Symptom | Duration | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Confusion** | Minutes to hours | Disorientation, inability to follow commands, automatic behavior. Longer after bilateral tonic-clonic seizures. |
| **Fatigue / somnolence** | Hours to days | Profound exhaustion is one of the most common and disabling post-ictal symptoms. |
| **Headache** | Hours | Post-ictal headache occurs in ~50% of patients, often migrainous. |
| **Todd's paresis** | Minutes to 36 hours | Transient weakness of the limbs involved in the seizure. Lateralizes to the seizure focus. Can mimic stroke. |
| **Aphasia** | Minutes to hours | Post-ictal language difficulty suggests dominant hemisphere involvement. |
| **Memory gap** | Permanent for ictal period | Patients have no memory of the seizure itself (if awareness was impaired) and may have anterograde amnesia for a period after. |
| **Psychosis** | Rare, hours to days | Post-ictal psychosis occurs in 2–6% of patients, typically 24–72 hours after a seizure cluster. |
| **Mood disturbance** | Hours to days | Depression, anxiety, or irritability following seizures. |

### Patient Experience vs. Observer Experience

| What the patient feels | What an observer sees |
|---|---|
| Aura symptoms (déjà vu, rising sensation, fear) | May appear normal or stare blankly |
| Loss of awareness — no memory of event | Automatisms, staring, unresponsiveness |
| Confusion, disorientation upon recovery | Wandering, inappropriate responses, aggression if restrained |
| Exhaustion, headache, emotional distress | Sleepiness, slow responses, pale or flushed |
| No recollection of convulsive phase | Tonic stiffening, clonic jerking, cyanosis, incontinence |

## 1.5 Triggers and Risk Factors

Evidence strength ratings: **Strong** = supported by multiple controlled studies and clinical guidelines; **Moderate** = consistent observational evidence; **Weak/Variable** = anecdotal, case reports, or conflicting data.

| Trigger | Evidence | Mechanism / Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Sleep deprivation** | Strong | One of the most consistently identified triggers. Even one night of poor sleep significantly lowers seizure threshold. Shift work and irregular sleep–wake cycles are high-risk. |
| **Missed medication** | Strong | The most common cause of breakthrough seizures. Missing even a single dose of a short-half-life ASM (e.g., carbamazepine, oxcarbazepine) can provoke a seizure. |
| **Alcohol** | Strong | Moderate-to-heavy consumption and withdrawal both lower seizure threshold. Withdrawal seizures typically occur 6–48 hours after the last drink. Binge drinking is higher risk than moderate regular use. |
| **Stress (psychological)** | Moderate–Strong | Self-reported as the most common trigger in patient surveys (reported by 50–70% of patients). Mechanism likely involves cortisol, norepinephrine effects on neuronal excitability, and sleep disruption. |
| **Illness / fever** | Moderate | Systemic infection, fever, and the associated metabolic changes lower seizure threshold. GI illness can impair medication absorption. |
| **Menstrual cycle (catamenial epilepsy)** | Moderate | Approximately one-third of women with epilepsy have seizure patterns linked to the menstrual cycle. Perimenstrual (C1 pattern) and periovulatory (C2 pattern) seizure exacerbation are recognized. Progesterone is anticonvulsant; estrogen is proconvulsant. |
| **Dehydration** | Moderate | Dehydration alters electrolyte balance (sodium, potassium), which can increase neuronal excitability. Common in hot weather, during illness, or with certain medications. |
| **Skipped meals / hypoglycemia** | Moderate | Low blood glucose can lower seizure threshold. Regular meal timing is recommended across guidelines. The ketogenic diet works through a different mechanism (ketosis) and does not lower blood sugar to dangerous levels when properly managed. |
| **Photosensitivity (photic stimulation)** | Moderate (for susceptible individuals) | Affects ~3–5% of people with epilepsy. Flashing lights at 15–25 Hz are the most provocative frequency. More common in generalized than focal epilepsy, but can trigger focal seizures in some patients. |
| **Specific stimuli (reflex seizures)** | Weak–Moderate | Reading, music, hot water, startle, specific cognitive tasks. These are relatively rare and patient-specific. |
| **Hyperventilation** | Moderate | Induces hypocapnia (low CO₂), cerebral vasoconstriction, and alkalosis, all of which can provoke seizures. More reliably triggers absence seizures than focal seizures. |
| **Recreational drugs** | Moderate | Cocaine, amphetamines, MDMA, and synthetic cannabinoids are proconvulsant. Cannabis evidence is mixed — CBD has anticonvulsant properties, THC effects are unclear. |
| **Medication interactions** | Moderate | Many common medications lower seizure threshold: tramadol, bupropion, certain antibiotics (fluoroquinolones, carbapenems), theophylline, high-dose antihistamines. ASM levels can be reduced by enzyme-inducing drugs. |

## 1.6 Effects and Burden

### Cognitive Effects

Cognitive impairment is one of the most troubling aspects of epilepsy for patients, arising from the epilepsy itself, its underlying cause, and medication side effects:

- **Memory**: The most commonly affected domain, especially in temporal lobe epilepsy. Encoding (forming new memories) and retrieval are both impaired. Hippocampal involvement is the primary driver.
- **Attention and processing speed**: Reduced sustained attention and slower processing are common, worsened by sedating ASMs (phenobarbital, benzodiazepines, topiramate).
- **Language**: Word-finding difficulty (anomia) is common in dominant-hemisphere temporal lobe epilepsy. Worsened post-ictally.
- **Executive function**: Planning, organization, and cognitive flexibility may be impaired, especially with frontal lobe epilepsy or diffuse effects of medications.

### Psychiatric Comorbidity

Psychiatric disorders are substantially more common in people with epilepsy than the general population:

| Condition | Prevalence in epilepsy | General population | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Depression | 20–55% | 7–10% | Bidirectional relationship — depression also increases the risk of developing epilepsy. ILAE recommends routine screening. |
| Anxiety disorders | 10–30% | 5–10% | Includes generalized anxiety, panic disorder, and seizure-specific anxiety (fear of next seizure). |
| ADHD | 20–40% (higher in childhood) | 5–7% | Stimulant medications are generally safe in epilepsy at therapeutic doses. |
| Psychosis | 2–7% | ~1% | Includes inter-ictal and post-ictal psychosis. |
| Suicidality | 2–5× increased risk | — | FDA black box warning on ASMs regarding suicidality. Screen actively. |

### Social, Occupational, and Legal

- **Driving**: Most jurisdictions require a seizure-free period (typically 3–12 months, varies by state/country) before driving is permitted. Loss of driving is consistently rated as one of the most impactful consequences of epilepsy.
- **Employment**: Higher unemployment rates. Workplace accommodations, stigma, and concerns about safety in certain occupations.
- **Relationships and social isolation**: Stigma remains a significant barrier. Many patients conceal their diagnosis.
- **Injury risk**: Falls, burns, drowning (bathtub seizures are particularly dangerous), and motor vehicle accidents. Injury risk is highest during bilateral tonic-clonic seizures.

### SUDEP (Sudden Unexpected Death in Epilepsy)

SUDEP is the sudden, unexpected, non-traumatic, non-drowning death of a person with epilepsy, with or without evidence of a seizure, excluding documented status epilepticus, where postmortem examination does not reveal a structural or toxicologic cause of death (Nashef definition).

- **Incidence**: ~1.2 per 1,000 patient-years in all epilepsy; up to 9 per 1,000 patient-years in drug-resistant epilepsy (Epilepsia, Harden et al., 2017).
- **Risk factors**: Frequent generalized tonic-clonic seizures (the strongest risk factor), nocturnal seizures, young adult age, long duration of epilepsy, polytherapy (as a proxy for severity), prone sleeping position post-seizure.
- **Protective factors**: Nighttime supervision, seizure detection devices, seizure control, and sleeping supine. AAN/AES guidelines (2017) recommend clinicians discuss SUDEP risk with patients.
- **Mechanism**: Likely post-ictal respiratory depression and/or cardiac arrhythmia. The MORTEMUS study (Lancet Neurology, 2013) showed that SUDEP followed GTCS, with terminal apnea preceding cardiac arrest.

## 1.7 Diagnosis and Monitoring

### Clinical History

The most important diagnostic tool. A detailed seizure history from the patient *and* a witness includes:
- Description of the event (onset, progression, duration, recovery)
- Level of awareness during the event
- Provocative factors
- Frequency, clustering, and temporal patterns
- Past medical history, family history of seizures/epilepsy
- Medication history, substance use

### Electroencephalography (EEG)

| Type | Setting | Duration | Yield | Use Case |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Routine EEG** | Office/lab | 20–40 min | ~50% show IEDs on first study | Initial workup. Sleep deprivation and hyperventilation increase yield. |
| **Ambulatory EEG** | Home | 24–72 hours | Higher yield than routine; captures sleep | When routine EEG is normal but suspicion is high; correlating symptoms with EEG patterns. |
| **Video-EEG monitoring** | Epilepsy monitoring unit | Days to weeks | Gold standard | Pre-surgical evaluation, PNES vs. epilepsy, seizure localization, medication adjustment. |
| **Intracranial EEG** | Surgical (invasive) | Days to weeks | Definitive localization | When non-invasive workup is insufficient for surgical planning. Stereo-EEG (sEEG) or subdural grids. |

### Neuroimaging

- **MRI brain (epilepsy protocol)**: 3T preferred. Specific sequences include thin-slice coronal FLAIR and T2 through the hippocampi, volumetric T1 for cortical analysis, and FLAIR for subtle lesions. Standard MRI protocols miss many epileptogenic lesions.
- **PET (FDG)**: Identifies areas of interictal hypometabolism corresponding to the seizure focus. Useful when MRI is non-lesional.
- **Ictal SPECT**: Injection during a seizure shows increased blood flow at the seizure onset zone. Requires rapid injection in the EMU.
- **MEG (magnetoencephalography)**: Localizes epileptiform sources with high spatial resolution. Available at specialized centers.
- **fMRI**: Functional mapping of language and memory (Wada test replacement) for surgical planning.

### Seizure Diaries

Paper or digital logs of seizure events, with date/time, type, duration, triggers, and context. Despite known limitations (underreporting, recall bias), seizure diaries remain the clinical standard for tracking seizure frequency and treatment response. Digital diaries and apps can improve accuracy and data richness.

### Wearables and Detection Devices

| Device / Technology | What It Detects | FDA Status | Evidence Level |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Wrist-worn accelerometry + EDA** (e.g., Embrace2/EmbracePlus by Empafit) | Bilateral tonic-clonic seizures via motion patterns and electrodermal changes | FDA-cleared (GTCS detection) | Validated for GTCS. Does not reliably detect focal seizures without motor components. |
| **Surface EMG** (e.g., Epi-Care Free, SPEAC) | Tonic-clonic seizures via muscle activity | FDA-cleared / CE marked (varies by device) | Good sensitivity for GTCS; limited for focal seizures. |
| **Under-mattress sensors** (e.g., Emfit, SAMi) | Nocturnal convulsive seizures via bed movement | Consumer devices, not FDA-cleared as medical devices | Useful for nocturnal monitoring. High false-positive rates. |
| **EEG headbands / behind-ear devices** (e.g., Epitel REMI, Ceribell, investigational devices) | All seizure types via EEG | Some FDA-cleared for EEG recording; seizure detection algorithms vary | Highest potential for detecting non-convulsive seizures. Comfort and signal quality remain challenges. |
| **Smartwatch heart rate** (Apple Watch, Fitbit, Garmin) | Ictal tachycardia (indirect seizure marker) | Consumer wellness features, not cleared as seizure detectors | Heart rate changes accompany many seizures. Emerging research on seizure detection algorithms. |

### Laboratory Monitoring

- **ASM drug levels**: Therapeutic drug monitoring for medications with narrow therapeutic indices (phenytoin, carbamazepine, valproate) or to assess adherence.
- **Metabolic panels**: Electrolytes (sodium — relevant for oxcarbazepine-induced hyponatremia), liver function (valproate, carbamazepine), kidney function, CBC.
- **Medication-specific monitoring**: Valproate → LFTs, ammonia, platelet count; carbamazepine → CBC (aplastic anemia risk), sodium; lamotrigine → rash monitoring; levetiracetam → behavioral changes.

## 1.8 Treatment and Mitigation

### a. Anti-Seizure Medications (ASMs)

ASMs are the first-line treatment for focal epilepsy. The goal is complete seizure freedom (achieved in ~50% with the first ASM, ~65% with two ASMs, and a small additional fraction with further trials). Approximately 30–35% of patients have drug-resistant epilepsy.

#### First-Line ASMs for Focal Seizures

| Medication | Mechanism Class | Evidence Level | Key Considerations |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Levetiracetam (Keppra)** | SV2A binding | ILAE Level A (first-line) | Broad-spectrum; minimal drug interactions; renal clearance. Side effects: irritability, behavioral changes ("Keppra rage"), fatigue. |
| **Lamotrigine (Lamictal)** | Sodium channel blockade | ILAE Level A (first-line) | Broad-spectrum; favorable cognitive profile; weight-neutral. Requires slow titration due to rash risk (Stevens-Johnson syndrome). Complex pharmacokinetics with hormonal contraceptives and valproate. |
| **Oxcarbazepine (Trileptal)** | Sodium channel blockade | ILAE Level A (first-line) | Effective for focal seizures. Risk of hyponatremia (monitor sodium). Better tolerated than carbamazepine. |
| **Carbamazepine (Tegretol)** | Sodium channel blockade | ILAE Level A (first-line) | Long track record for focal seizures. Enzyme inducer (affects other drug levels). HLA-B*1502 testing recommended before use in certain populations (risk of SJS/TEN). |

#### Second-Line and Adjunctive ASMs for Focal Seizures

| Medication | Mechanism Class | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Lacosamide (Vimpat)** | Sodium channel (slow inactivation) | Well-tolerated; available IV. May cause PR interval prolongation. |
| **Zonisamide (Zonegran)** | Multiple (sodium/calcium channels, carbonic anhydrase) | Effective adjunct; weight loss. Risk of kidney stones, cognitive effects, heat intolerance. |
| **Topiramate (Topamax)** | Multiple (sodium channels, GABA, glutamate, carbonic anhydrase) | Effective but significant cognitive side effects ("Dopamax" — word-finding difficulty, mental slowing). Weight loss. Teratogenic — avoid in women of childbearing potential if possible. |
| **Brivaracetam (Briviact)** | SV2A binding (higher affinity than levetiracetam) | Alternative to levetiracetam with potentially fewer behavioral side effects. |
| **Perampanel (Fycompa)** | AMPA receptor antagonist | Novel mechanism. Can cause aggression, dizziness. Take at bedtime. |
| **Eslicarbazepine (Aptiom)** | Sodium channel blockade | Once-daily dosing. Similar efficacy to oxcarbazepine with potentially fewer side effects. |
| **Clobazam (Onfi)** | Benzodiazepine (GABA-A) | Effective adjunct; less sedating than other benzodiazepines. Tolerance can develop. |
| **Cenobamate (Xcopri)** | Sodium channel + GABA-A modulation | Highly effective in drug-resistant focal epilepsy (ILAE, 2023 guidelines update). Requires very slow titration. |
| **Valproate (Depakote)** | Multiple (sodium channels, GABA, HDAC inhibition) | Broad-spectrum. Highly teratogenic — contraindicated in women of childbearing potential unless no alternative. Weight gain, tremor, hair loss, hepatotoxicity. |
| **Phenytoin (Dilantin)** | Sodium channel blockade | Oldest ASM still in use. Non-linear pharmacokinetics make dosing difficult. Gingival hyperplasia, osteoporosis, cosmetic effects with long-term use. Generally reserved for acute settings or patients already stable on it. |

#### Rescue / Abortive Medications

For seizure clusters or prolonged seizures outside the hospital:

| Medication | Route | Onset | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Diazepam rectal gel (Diastat)** | Rectal | 5–15 min | FDA-approved for seizure clusters. Practical barriers to use. |
| **Midazolam nasal spray (Nayzilam)** | Intranasal | 5–10 min | FDA-approved 2019. More socially acceptable than rectal diazepam. |
| **Diazepam nasal spray (Valtoco)** | Intranasal | 5–15 min | FDA-approved 2020. Alternative nasal option. |

#### Drug-Resistant (Refractory) Epilepsy

**Definition (ILAE, 2010)**: Failure of adequate trials of two tolerated, appropriately chosen, and appropriately dosed ASM schedules (whether as monotherapy or in combination) to achieve sustained seizure freedom.

Approximately 30–35% of people with epilepsy are drug-resistant. This is not merely inconvenient — drug-resistant epilepsy carries higher mortality (including SUDEP), greater cognitive decline, more injuries, and worse quality of life. Patients meeting this definition should be referred to a comprehensive epilepsy center for evaluation of surgical and non-pharmacological options.

### b. Surgical and Ablative Treatments

Surgery is the most effective treatment for drug-resistant focal epilepsy when a surgically accessible focus can be identified.

| Procedure | Indication | Seizure-Free Rate | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Anterior temporal lobectomy** | Mesial temporal lobe epilepsy with hippocampal sclerosis | 60–80% seizure-free at 1 year (ILAE, Engel Class I) | The best-studied epilepsy surgery. Randomized trial evidence (Wiebe et al., NEJM 2001) demonstrates superiority over medical therapy alone. |
| **Lesionectomy** | Focal lesion (tumor, FCD, cavernoma) visible on MRI | 50–80% depending on lesion type and complete resection | Outcomes best when the lesion can be completely removed. |
| **Laser interstitial thermal therapy (LITT)** | Mesial temporal structures, deep lesions, hypothalamic hamartoma | 50–60% seizure-free | Minimally invasive (MRI-guided laser ablation). Shorter hospital stay than open surgery. Growing use for mesial temporal lobe epilepsy. |
| **Corpus callosotomy** | Drop attacks (atonic/tonic seizures) — primarily generalized epilepsy | Reduces drop attacks in 60–80%; rarely seizure-free | Not typically for focal epilepsy. Palliative — reduces injury from falls. |
| **Hemispherectomy / hemispherotomy** | Extensive unilateral hemispheric disease (e.g., Rasmussen's encephalitis, hemimegalencephaly) | 70–85% seizure-free | Major surgery with significant functional trade-offs. Performed primarily in children with already-compromised hemisphere. |

### c. Dietary Therapies

| Diet | Description | Evidence for Focal Epilepsy | Practical Burden |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Classic ketogenic diet** | 4:1 or 3:1 fat-to-(protein+carb) ratio. Very high fat, very low carbohydrate. | Strong evidence in pediatric epilepsy; moderate evidence in adults. >50% seizure reduction in ~50% of patients who adhere. | High. Requires precise measurement, nutritionist supervision, regular lab monitoring (lipids, kidney function, growth in children). |
| **Modified Atkins diet (MAD)** | Restricts carbohydrates to 10–20 g/day without restricting fat or protein ratios. | Moderate evidence. Similar efficacy to classic keto in some studies with better tolerance and adherence. | Moderate. More flexible than classic keto. Better suited to adults. |
| **Low glycemic index treatment (LGIT)** | Allows 40–60 g/day of low-GI carbohydrates. | Moderate evidence. Less restrictive, easier to sustain. | Low–Moderate. Most practical option for long-term adult adherence. |
| **MCT (medium-chain triglyceride) diet** | Supplements medium-chain triglycerides to allow more carbohydrate/protein. | Moderate evidence. Comparable to classic keto. | Moderate. GI side effects from MCT oil. |

All dietary therapies should be supervised by a dietitian with epilepsy experience and coordinated with the patient's neurologist. They are adjunctive — not replacements for ASMs.

### d. Neurostimulation Devices

#### Implanted Devices

| Device | Mechanism | FDA Status | Evidence for Focal Seizures |
|---|---|---|---|
| **VNS (Vagus Nerve Stimulation)** — LivaNova | Intermittent electrical stimulation of the left vagus nerve via implanted generator. | FDA-approved since 1997 for drug-resistant focal seizures (age ≥4) | Reduces seizure frequency by ≥50% in ~50% of patients at 1 year. Efficacy may improve over 2–3 years. Not typically curative. Side effects: hoarseness, cough, throat tingling during stimulation. |
| **RNS (Responsive Neurostimulation)** — NeuroPace | Implanted neurostimulator detects abnormal EEG activity and delivers targeted stimulation to the seizure focus. Closed-loop system. | FDA-approved 2013 for adults with drug-resistant focal seizures with 1–2 identified foci | Median seizure reduction: 53% at 1 year, 66% at 2 years, with continued improvement over time. Records chronic EEG data. |
| **DBS (Deep Brain Stimulation of the Anterior Nucleus of the Thalamus)** — Medtronic | Continuous or scheduled stimulation of the anterior thalamic nuclei. | FDA-approved 2018 for drug-resistant focal seizures in adults | SANTE trial: 56% median seizure reduction at 2 years, 75% at 7 years. Side effects: depression, memory complaints (similar rates to sham in trial). |

#### Non-Invasive Neurostimulation

| Modality | Mechanism | FDA/Regulatory Status | Evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| **TMS (Transcranial Magnetic Stimulation)** | Repetitive magnetic pulses alter cortical excitability. Low-frequency rTMS over the seizure focus is inhibitory. | FDA-cleared for depression; **not** FDA-cleared for epilepsy. CE marked for epilepsy in some jurisdictions. | Mixed results. Some controlled trials show modest seizure reduction; others show no benefit. ILAE position: insufficient evidence for routine use (2020). |
| **tDCS (Transcranial Direct Current Stimulation)** | Weak constant current modulates cortical excitability. Cathodal stimulation reduces excitability. | **Investigational** for epilepsy. | Small pilot studies show promise. No large RCTs. Not recommended outside research settings. |
| **Transcranial focused ultrasound (tFUS)** | Focused ultrasound energy modulates deep brain targets non-invasively. | **Investigational**. | Early-phase trials for focal epilepsy. Potential to reach deep targets (hippocampus, thalamus) without surgery. |
| **External trigeminal nerve stimulation (eTNS)** — Monarch by NeuroSigma | External stimulation of trigeminal nerve branches via forehead electrodes. | FDA-cleared for ADHD (2019); **investigational** for epilepsy (CE marked in EU for epilepsy). | Limited evidence. One RCT showed >50% seizure reduction in 30% of treated patients vs. 21% with sham (DeGiorgio et al., Neurology 2013). |

#### Cognitive/Neuroplasticity Training — Separate Category

The following are sometimes discussed alongside neurostimulation but are fundamentally different in mechanism and evidence:

- **Neurofeedback (EEG biofeedback)**: Patient learns to modify their own EEG patterns (e.g., increase sensorimotor rhythm, reduce theta) through real-time feedback. Some uncontrolled studies report seizure reduction. A Cochrane review (2019) found insufficient evidence to recommend neurofeedback for epilepsy. It is adjunctive at best and not a proven seizure treatment.
- **Cognitive rehabilitation**: Structured training of memory, attention, and executive function. Addresses cognitive comorbidity, not seizure frequency. May improve quality of life and functional status but does not treat the underlying epilepsy.
- **Biofeedback (galvanic skin response, heart rate variability)**: Used for stress management. May indirectly reduce stress-triggered seizures. No direct anticonvulsant evidence.
- **Mindfulness and behavioral interventions**: Mindfulness-based stress reduction (MBSR) and cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) can reduce seizure-related anxiety and improve coping. Some evidence for modest seizure reduction in stress-responsive epilepsy (Epilepsy & Behavior, Tang et al., 2015).

**Critical clarification**: None of these constitute "neurocalisthenics" in the sense of electrical brain training that prevents seizures. Implanted devices (VNS, RNS, DBS) deliver therapeutic electrical stimulation. The cognitive/behavioral approaches listed above are psychological or rehabilitative interventions. They complement medical treatment but do not replace it.

### e. Forensic Options — Investigative, Diagnostic, and Adherence Verification

The term "forensic" here refers to the investigative and verification layer around epilepsy management: reconstructing what happened during an event, confirming the diagnosis, verifying adherence, and establishing objective data.

#### Seizure-Forensic Detection and Event Reconstruction

- **Video-EEG monitoring**: The gold standard for correlating clinical events with electrical brain activity. Determines whether an event is epileptic, PNES, or other.
- **Ambulatory EEG**: Captures events in the patient's natural environment over days.
- **Wearable data review**: Post-hoc analysis of accelerometry, EDA, and heart rate data from wearables to identify and characterize seizure-like events.
- **Smartphone/app-based event logging**: Immediate post-event recording (voice notes, video) while memory is fresh. Bystander video is clinically valuable — neurologists use it for semiological analysis.
- **Security camera / home video review**: Nocturnal seizures may be captured by bedside cameras (e.g., SAMi camera). These recordings help characterize seizure type, duration, and recovery.

#### Workup Forensics (Diagnostic Investigation)

- **Toxicology screen**: Rule out substance-induced seizures (sympathomimetics, withdrawal syndromes).
- **Metabolic panels**: Electrolytes (sodium, calcium, magnesium, glucose) to identify provoked seizures.
- **ASM drug-level assays**: Verify that serum levels are within therapeutic range. Low levels suggest non-adherence, malabsorption, or drug interactions.
- **Autoantibody panels**: Anti-NMDA receptor, anti-LGI1, anti-CASPR2, anti-GAD65 antibodies for suspected autoimmune epilepsy. Increasingly important in new-onset refractory epilepsy.
- **Genetic panels**: Epilepsy gene panels (next-generation sequencing) for patients with early-onset epilepsy, drug-resistant epilepsy, or features suggesting a genetic syndrome. Expanding role in precision medicine (e.g., SCN1A mutations guide away from sodium channel blockers).

#### Medication Adherence Forensics

Non-adherence is the most common and most modifiable cause of breakthrough seizures. Methods for assessment:

| Method | Reliability | Patient Acceptability | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Self-report** | Low (overestimates adherence) | High | Easy but systematically biased. |
| **Pill counts** | Low–Moderate | Moderate | Can be gamed. Doesn't confirm timing. |
| **Electronic pill monitoring** (e.g., MEMS caps, smart pill bottles) | Moderate–High | Moderate | Records opening times. Doesn't confirm ingestion. |
| **Drug-level assays** | High (for a point in time) | Low (requires blood draw) | Gold standard for objective adherence verification. Single time point — can miss intermittent non-adherence. |
| **Prescription refill records** | Moderate | High (passive) | Population-level data; doesn't confirm pill-taking behavior. |
| **Digital therapeutics / app-based tracking** | Moderate | High (if well-designed) | Patient-facing apps that prompt and record medication taking. The approach this application is designed to take. |

**Ethical framing**: A patient app should *support* adherence, not *surveil* it. Design principles: the data belongs to the patient; sharing with clinicians is opt-in; the tone is supportive, not punitive; the system acknowledges that non-adherence is often unintentional (forgetfulness, side effects, cost) rather than willful.

### f. Lifestyle, Routines, and Habit-Level Mitigation

These are the modifiable factors a patient can control daily, often with the most immediate impact on seizure frequency after medication adherence.

| Domain | Recommendation | Evidence / Rationale |
|---|---|---|
| **Sleep schedule** | Fixed bedtime and wake time, 7–9 hours, even on weekends. No rotating shift work if possible. | Sleep deprivation is one of the strongest and most consistent seizure triggers. (AAN, NICE, Epilepsy Foundation) |
| **Medication timing** | Take medications at the same time daily, anchored to existing routines (meals, brushing teeth). Use alarms/reminders. Never abruptly stop an ASM. | Consistent timing maintains steady drug levels. Abrupt withdrawal of most ASMs can trigger seizure clusters or status epilepticus. |
| **Meal timing** | Regular meals at consistent times. Avoid prolonged fasting. | Irregular eating patterns and hypoglycemia lower seizure threshold. |
| **Hydration** | Consistent fluid intake, ~2L/day (adjust for climate, activity, medication). Avoid overhydration (hyponatremia risk, especially with oxcarbazepine). | Dehydration alters electrolytes; overhydration can worsen hyponatremia in susceptible patients. |
| **Alcohol** | Limit to ≤1 drink/day or abstain. Avoid binge drinking. | Alcohol and withdrawal are direct proconvulsants. Interacts with many ASMs (sedation, impaired metabolism). |
| **Stress management** | Regular stress-reduction practice: mindfulness, progressive muscle relaxation, yoga, breathing exercises, CBT. | Stress is the most commonly self-reported trigger. Stress reduction shows modest evidence for seizure reduction. |
| **Exercise** | Regular moderate aerobic exercise (30 min/day most days). Safe environment — avoid unsupervised swimming, heights, contact sports depending on seizure control. | Exercise reduces stress, improves sleep, and has potential antiseizure effects (animal models). Not associated with increased seizure risk at moderate intensity. |
| **Photosensitivity precautions** | For photosensitive patients: avoid strobe lights, use screen dimming, wear polarized sunglasses, cover one eye in unavoidable situations (reduces bilateral visual cortex activation). | Evidence-based for the ~3–5% of people with epilepsy who have photosensitive seizures. |
| **Seizure action plan** | Written document with: seizure description, rescue medication instructions, when to call 911, emergency contacts, allergies, current medications. Share with household members, workplace, school. | AES/AAN recommendation. Reduces emergency response errors and provides critical information to first responders. |
| **Rescue plan** | Know where rescue medication is, who can administer it, and when (e.g., seizure lasting >X minutes). Practice with caregivers. | Guideline-recommended for patients with seizure clusters or prolonged seizures. |

## 1.9 Frontier / Pipeline

| Approach | Stage | Description |
|---|---|---|
| **Cenobamate optimization** | Approved (2019), expanding use | Already approved for focal seizures but considered a breakthrough given its high responder rate in drug-resistant epilepsy. Ongoing studies in broader populations. |
| **Fenfluramine (Fintepla)** | Approved for Dravet/LGS; Phase 3 for other epilepsies | Serotonin-modulating ASM. Currently approved for Dravet syndrome and Lennox-Gastaut; trials for focal epilepsy are ongoing. |
| **Antisense oligonucleotides (ASOs)** | Phase 1–2 | Gene-specific therapies targeting mRNA (e.g., STK-001 for SCN1A in Dravet syndrome). Represents the precision medicine approach — treating the genetic cause, not just symptoms. |
| **Gene therapy** | Preclinical to Phase 1 | AAV-vector delivery of genes to correct channelopathies or enzyme deficiencies. Still early stage for epilepsy; more advanced in other neurological disorders. |
| **Closed-loop neurostimulation (next-gen)** | Phase 2–3, some features approved | Building on RNS: AI-enhanced seizure prediction and preemptive stimulation. Improved algorithms for seizure forecasting. NeuroPace RNS already captures chronic data. |
| **AI seizure forecasting** | Research / early clinical | Machine learning models using chronic EEG (from RNS, wearables), heart rate variability, and other biosignals to predict seizure likelihood hours to days in advance. NeuroVista trial demonstrated feasibility; current efforts use smartphone-integrated algorithms. |
| **Precision medicine by genetic etiology** | Emerging clinical practice | Genotype-guided treatment: SCN1A → avoid sodium channel blockers; TSC → mTOR inhibitors (everolimus); DEPDC5 → possible mTOR pathway. Genetic testing increasingly guides ASM selection. |
| **Novel ASM mechanisms** | Phase 1–3 | Positive allosteric modulators of GABA-A (ganaxolone — approved for CDKL5), potassium channel openers (XEN1101, Phase 3), novel glutamate targets, neuroinflammation targets. |
| **Focused ultrasound ablation** | Phase 1–2 for epilepsy | MRI-guided focused ultrasound for lesion ablation without craniotomy. More established in essential tremor; early trials for epilepsy. |
| **Cannabidiol (Epidiolex) in focal epilepsy** | Approved for Dravet/LGS/TSC; research for focal | Purified CBD is FDA-approved for specific syndromes. Evidence for focal epilepsy in adults is limited but under investigation. |

## 1.10 Safety and Scope

This document is an educational reference compiled from published clinical guidelines, peer-reviewed literature, and institutional resources. It is **not medical advice** and does not replace the judgment of a treating physician.

**Key safety statements:**
- Every medication, surgical, dietary, and device-based treatment discussed here requires supervision by a qualified neurologist or epileptologist.
- Dosages are intentionally omitted. ASM dosing is individualized based on seizure type, weight, renal/hepatic function, drug interactions, and tolerability.
- No treatment should be started, stopped, or changed based on this document. Abrupt ASM withdrawal is dangerous.
- Emerging and investigational treatments are labeled as such. Their inclusion does not constitute endorsement.
- SUDEP risk should be discussed with a neurologist. Seizure control is the most important modifiable factor.
